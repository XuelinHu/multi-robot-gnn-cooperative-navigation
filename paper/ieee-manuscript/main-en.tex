\begin{abstract}
Multi-robot navigation in shared space requires simultaneous goal reaching,
coordination, and collision avoidance under a communication topology that
changes at every control step. We study whether a shared message-passing
policy over a dynamic communication graph can deliver such behavior, and we
report where it fails. Robots are modelled as graph nodes, edges are
established by a communication radius, and each directed edge carries a
five-dimensional relative-motion feature that encodes normalized relative
position, relative velocity, and closing speed. Policies are trained by
imitation from an ORCA-style expert with a joint objective combining action,
one-step separation, goal-progress, and smoothness terms, and are optionally
followed by an external pairwise safety filter. We evaluate A* grid tracking,
ORCA-style and RVO-style local controllers, an independent per-robot MLP, a
message-passing GNN, a neighbor-attention GNN, and a graph transformer over
four map families, 5--40 robots, and 30 unseen test seeds, with ROS~2,
Gazebo, and RViz2 integration verified on a Wheeltec platform. Message
passing improves task completion over an edge-free policy of identical
capacity (0.725 versus 0.425 success at 10 robots), but the mean-aggregation
GNN does not surpass the independent MLP in the main comparison, and adding
neighbor attention lowers success to 0.342. Multi-scale training with edge
features raises goal success to 0.958--0.967 at 5--10 robots under safety
filtering, yet strict no-physical-collision success falls from 0.708 at 20
robots to 0.250 at 30 and 0.000 at 40. We therefore identify 10--20 robots as
the practical operating range of the current system and treat higher
densities as a stress test that exposes a safety bottleneck which imitation
learning alone does not remove.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Cooperative navigation asks a group of robots to reach assigned goals in a
shared workspace without colliding with each other or with static obstacles.
Two properties make this harder than single-robot planning: the joint state
space grows with the population, and the influence each robot has on the
others is time-varying, since neighbors continuously enter and leave sensing
range. Centralized joint search stays exact but scales poorly, while fully
independent local policies discard the interaction structure that makes the
problem tractable at all.

Graph neural networks (GNNs) offer a middle ground. A robot group can be
represented as a graph whose nodes are robots and whose edges are
communication relations, and message passing then aggregates neighbor
information while sharing parameters across nodes. Because the same weights
apply at every node, one policy can serve a variable number of robots; because
edges are rebuilt from current positions, the representation adapts to a
changing topology.

This paper asks two questions. Given identical observations, action limits,
and termination rules, does an explicit local communication graph improve a
learned policy relative to one that sees only its own node features? And how
do communication range and an external safety filter trade off among goal
completion, collision risk, and per-step computation?

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a reproducible framework unifying scenario generation, classical and
  learned baselines, training, evaluation, and visualization under one data
  contract;
  \item a message-passing policy with five-dimensional relative-motion edge
  features, a joint imitation and separation objective, and an external
  pairwise safety filter ablated separately from the policy;
  \item a comparison spanning A*, ORCA-style, RVO-style, an independent MLP, a
  mean-aggregation GNN, a neighbor-attention GNN, and a graph transformer over
  four map families, 5--40 robots, and 30 unseen seeds, with edge, radius, and
  safety ablations;
  \item a ROS~2, Gazebo, and RViz2 integration on a Wheeltec platform
  validating namespaced odometry, LiDAR, velocity-command, and marker topics.
\end{itemize}

We do not frame the results as a general superiority claim for GNNs. Several
measurements contradict that reading, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports
them as measured.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Classical and Rule-Based Planning}
A* searches a discretized grid heuristically and is complete on a static map,
but coordinating one path per robot reintroduces combinatorial coupling
\cite{hart1968}. Velocity-obstacle methods instead reason in velocity space,
rejecting a candidate velocity if the relative motion it induces would
intersect a neighbor's trajectory \cite{fiorini1998}. They are cheap and
geometrically interpretable, but depend strongly on the time horizon and on
the reciprocity assumed between agents.

\subsection{Learned Multi-Robot Collision Avoidance}
Decentralized deep reinforcement learning removes joint search by acting from
local observations, though dense interactions outside the training
distribution remain a failure mode \cite{long2018orcamarl, thumiger2022uav}.
Curriculum learning grows the difficulty of constrained passages
\cite{komol2025curriculum}, physics-informed priors encourage safe behavior
\cite{feng2024physics}, and semi-supervised variants cut the labeling
requirement \cite{parooei2023semisupervised}. Vessel and formation
applications show that communication and shape constraints fold into the same
learning problem \cite{niu2023ship, wang2025ships}.

\subsection{Graph Representations for Robot Teams}
Diao \textit{et al.} encode spatial relations in a graph for path planning
\cite{diao2024gnn}, and He \textit{et al.} pair graph attention with
hierarchical motion planning to select the neighbors that matter
\cite{he2023ganav}. Goarin and Loianno use a GNN for decentralized goal
assignment, which suits variable team sizes but does not address continuous
avoidance \cite{goarin2024assignment}, and Yang \textit{et al.} couple a GNN
with PPO for multi-UAV planning \cite{yang2025uav}. Surveys note the recurring
tension that rule-based methods are interpretable but brittle while learned
methods are flexible but lack hard guarantees \cite{yang2025survey}.

Our design follows that tension's suggested compromise: a graph policy learns
cooperative motion and a separate filter handles close-range closure risk. We
ablate the two independently and report strict physical-collision metrics for
each rather than attributing filtered behavior to the network. We also note
that reported gains are frequently established at a single population size on
a few maps; the multi-scale stress test below probes that gap.

\section{Problem Formulation}

Let $\mathcal{V}=\{1,\dots,N\}$ index the robots, with position
$p_i\in\mathbb{R}^2$, velocity $v_i\in\mathbb{R}^2$, and goal
$g_i\in\mathbb{R}^2$. A directed communication graph is rebuilt at every step
from a radius $R_c$:
\begin{equation}
A_{ij}=
\begin{cases}
1, & i\neq j,\ \|p_i-p_j\|\le R_c,\\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Node features are eight-dimensional: goal-relative position, normalized
velocity, direction and distance to the nearest obstacle, and a bias term. The
policy emits a two-dimensional local velocity $u_i$ with
$\|u_i\|\le v_{\max}=1.4$~m/s, and positions evolve as
$p_i^{t+1}=p_i^t+\Delta t\,u_i^t$ with $\Delta t=0.08$~s.

Two distance thresholds are used throughout and never conflated. A
\emph{safety violation} occurs when any pair is closer than $d_s=0.72$~m; a
\emph{physical collision} occurs when any pair is closer than the sum of the
robot radii, $0.56$~m. A \emph{collision event} is counted each time a pair
transitions from a non-violating to a violating state, so a sustained overlap
contributes one event rather than one per step. Task success requires the
final mean goal error across all robots to fall below a fixed threshold.

To keep quantities of different scale comparable inside the network, define
the normalized operator $\bar{x}=x/s_x$. For a directed edge $(j,i)$ we use
the five-dimensional feature
\begin{equation}
e_{ij}^t=\left[\frac{p_i^t-p_j^t}{[W,H]},\ \frac{v_i^t-v_j^t}{v_{\max}},\
\frac{-\big((v_i^t-v_j^t)\cdot\hat{r}_{ij}^t\big)}{v_{\max}}\right],
\end{equation}
with $\hat{r}_{ij}^t=(p_i^t-p_j^t)/(\|p_i^t-p_j^t\|+\epsilon)$ and $[W,H]$ the
map extents. The final component is the closing speed, positive precisely when
the pair is approaching, so the network can condition on motion tendency rather
than instantaneous distance alone.

\section{Method}

\subsection{System Overview}
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the closed loop. Each robot is a node, edges follow
the communication radius, and the encoder, message-passing layers, and action
head share parameters across nodes, so one policy handles any population size
without retraining. Observations are assembled from the current state, the
graph is rebuilt, and the safety filter optionally corrects the emitted
velocity before execution.

\subsection{Baselines}
We implement A* grid tracking, an ORCA-style local repulsion controller, and a
dependency-free RVO-style controller that enumerates candidate velocities over
a finite lattice of headings and speed levels, scoring each by predicted
neighbor clearance, obstacle occupancy, and goal progress. Because it exists to
give every method a common substrate, we call it RVO-style and claim no
equivalence to a formally verified RVO reference library. The independent MLP
applies the same perceptron to every node and never receives the adjacency
matrix, isolating the contribution of explicit interaction modelling from that
of the node features themselves.

\subsection{Message-Passing Policy}
A linear layer first maps node features to a hidden representation. Each
layer averages neighbor representations and updates the node from its own
state concatenated with the transformed neighborhood message:
\begin{equation}
m_i^{(l)}=\tfrac{1}{\max(1,d_i)}{\textstyle\sum_j}A_{ij}h_j^{(l)},\;
h_i^{(l+1)}=\sigma(W_l[h_i^{(l)}\Vert\phi_l(m_i^{(l)})]).
\end{equation}
With edge features the message becomes
\begin{equation}
m_i^{(l)}=\tfrac{1}{\max(1,d_i)}{\textstyle\sum_j}A_{ij}(\phi_l(h_j^{(l)})+\psi_l(e_{ij})),
\end{equation}
followed by
$h_i^{(l+1)}=\mathrm{ReLU}(W_l[h_i^{(l)}\Vert m_i^{(l)}])$. We use three
message-passing layers, 128 hidden units, and a shared action head, with the
action bounded by $\hat{u}_i=v_{\max}\tanh(f_\theta(h_i))$.

\subsection{Attention Variants}
To test whether neighbor weighting or global self-attention changes the
picture, we add two models with identical inputs, outputs, data, and test
scenarios. The neighbor-attention GNN replaces mean aggregation with
multi-head attention over queries, keys, and values, still masked by the local
adjacency. The graph transformer uses multi-head self-attention with the
dynamic adjacency as mask, residual connections, and feed-forward blocks. Both
use 128 hidden units, four heads, and a shared policy head.

\subsection{Training Objective}
Pure action imitation reproduces the expert's local behavior but does not
guarantee a safe closed loop, so we train on a weighted sum of four terms:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\lambda_a\mathcal{L}_{act}+\lambda_c\mathcal{L}_{col}
+\lambda_p\mathcal{L}_{prog}+\lambda_s\mathcal{L}_{smooth},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{act}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_i
\mathrm{Huber}(u_i,u_i^*)$ is the imitation term. Writing the one-step
predicted pair distance as $d_{ij}'=\|p_i+\Delta t\,u_i-p_j-\Delta t\,u_j\|$,
$\mathcal{L}_{col}=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}[d_s-d_{ij}']_+^2$ penalizes
predicted separation. The remaining terms are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{prog}&=\tfrac{1}{N}{\textstyle\sum_i}[\|g_i-p_i'\|-\|g_i-p_i\|]_+,\\
\mathcal{L}_{smooth}&=\tfrac{1}{N}{\textstyle\sum_i}\|u_i^t-u_i^{t-1}\|_2^2,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $[x]_+=\max(x,0)$. Padding nodes are masked by $q_i\in\{0,1\}$, so every
node loss becomes $\sum_i q_i\ell_i/\max(1,\sum_i q_i)$ and a graph padded to
40 nodes trains on exactly the real robots in it.

Multi-scale training samples the 5--40 robot datasets from all four map
families and pads each graph to 40 nodes. The progress and smoothness terms
matter in practice: a separation penalty alone reduces goal progress, so its
weight must be scheduled or paired with $\mathcal{L}_{prog}$.

\subsection{Safety Filter}
After the policy emits its action, the filter inspects every close pair and
applies a symmetric correction before clipping. Let
$n_{ij}=\delta_{ij}/(\|\delta_{ij}\|+\epsilon)$ with $\delta_{ij}=p_i-p_j$. If
$\|\delta_{ij}\|<d_s+\tau$ and $(u_i-u_j)\cdot n_{ij}<0$, then
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{u}_i&=u_i+\tfrac{\kappa_{ij}}{2}n_{ij},\quad
\tilde{u}_j=u_j-\tfrac{\kappa_{ij}}{2}n_{ij},\\
\kappa_{ij}&=-(u_i-u_j)\cdot n_{ij}+[d_s+\tau-\|\delta_{ij}\|]_+,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
after which each $\tilde{u}_i$ is clipped to $v_{\max}$. The module runs only
at inference and in the ROS control path, and Section~\ref{sec:results}
reports its effect separately from the policy's.

\section{Experimental Setup}

Experiments span four map families---\textit{crossing}, \textit{open},
\textit{random}, and \textit{narrow}---and 5, 10, 20, 30, and 40 robots.
Development data uses fixed seeds with 20 episodes of at most 160 steps each.
The frozen main evaluation covers $4\times3\times5\times10=600$ episodes over
three robot counts, five methods, and ten fixed test seeds; the extended
learned-model comparison covers 480 episodes for four learned policies; and
the multi-scale test covers 1800 safety-filtered and 1800 unfiltered episodes
over 30 unseen seeds (100--129). All methods share initial states, goals,
obstacles, action limits, and termination rules. Data are stored as compressed
NPZ archives holding features, adjacency, actions, positions, rewards,
obstacles, layout, expert identifier, and step index.

Reported metrics are goal success rate, strict no-physical-collision success
rate, safety-violation events, physical collision events, minimum inter-robot
separation, path length, final goal error, and mean per-step control time.
Means, standard deviations, and 95\% confidence intervals are computed over
episodes, and violations at $0.72$~m and physical collisions at $0.56$~m are
always reported in separate columns. Training used one 24~GB GPU with batch
size 128.

\subsection{Main Comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:main} aggregates the frozen 600-episode comparison. The
classical controllers are strong: A* reaches every goal, and RVO-style reaches
0.975--1.000 success with the shortest paths, but its control time grows almost
two orders of magnitude across the range, from 7.8~ms at 5 robots to 87.4~ms
at 20. ORCA-style keeps the largest minimum separation (0.396~m at 5 robots)
at the lowest per-step cost, though some narrow passages stall it. The
independent MLP is the strongest learned policy here at 0.725--0.875 success.

The mean-aggregation GNN does not beat that MLP. It reaches 0.625, 0.725, and
0.700 success at 5, 10, and 20 robots with comparable collision-event counts,
and its path lengths and control times are the highest among learned methods.
This checkpoint was trained on 10-robot data only, so the 5 and 20-robot
columns are cross-scale generalization rather than matched training
conditions. The conclusion we draw is narrow but firm: on this benchmark,
adding a communication graph is not by itself sufficient to outperform an
independent policy, and success rate or path length alone would not justify a
superiority claim.

\subsection{Attention and Transformer Variants}
Table~\ref{tab:learned} compares four learned policies on 480 episodes.
Overall goal success is 0.817 for the MLP, 0.683 for the GNN, 0.558 for the
graph transformer, and 0.342 for the neighbor-attention GNN; collision-free
success under the 0.56~m threshold is 0.017, 0.008, 0.058, and 0.025. Two
observations follow. The transformer roughly doubles the GNN's strict success
while costing about 1.6$\times$ the control time, so expressiveness helps even
while the safety outcome stays poor. But adding attention in its
neighbor-masked form hurts badly, cutting success to 0.342 and raising control
time to 1.298~ms. Attention is not a free improvement, and no variant here is

\subsection{Graph Structure Ablation}
Removing edges isolates the contribution of message passing
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ablations}a). At 10 robots the local-graph policy reaches 0.725
success against 0.425 for the edge-free policy of identical capacity; at 20
robots the figures are 0.700 and 0.425, and at 5 robots 0.625 against 0.500.
The global graph, connecting every pair regardless of distance, does not
improve on the local graph and is worse at 10 robots (0.625 versus 0.725).
Message passing therefore contributes real coordination ability, but a denser
graph is not automatically better and neither variant removes collisions.

\subsection{Safety Filter Ablation}
The safety filter is evaluated on its own against the raw policy on 60
episodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablations}b). At 10 robots it reduces mean collision
events from 4.900 to 0.200 and raises mean minimum separation from 0.069~m to
0.794~m, but success drops from 1.000 to 0.900. At 20 robots collision events
fall from 27.500 to 6.900 with success falling from 0.900 to 0.700, and
separation reaches only 0.067~m, so no stable collision-free arrival is
achieved at that density. The filter also leaves some robots stalled rather
than rerouting them. The trade is direct: separation improves substantially
while task completion degrades, which is our main reason for arguing that the
safety constraint belongs in training rather than in a post-hoc projection.

\subsection{Communication Radius}
Larger radii increase mean graph degree and supply more neighbors at the cost
of more aggregation work. At 10 robots a radius of 2.0~m yields only 0.200
success with mean degree 0.463, while 3.4~m and 5.5~m both reach 1.000 success
with degrees 2.283 and 4.420; the global graph reaches 0.900 with degree
9.000. At 5 robots success climbs monotonically from 0.100 at 2.0~m to 1.000
for the global graph, indicating that sparse graphs simply disconnect small
teams. Control time stays near 1.0~ms across radii, so the connectivity gain
is nearly free below 20 robots; the global graph does not, however, eliminate
physical collisions.

\subsection{Multi-Scale Stress Test}
Table~\ref{tab:multiscale} reports the 1800-episode safety-filtered test over
30 unseen seeds, and it is the most informative result in this paper. Goal
success is high and stable at 5--20 robots: 0.967, 0.958, and 0.758 for the
GNN and 0.983, 1.000, and 0.842 for the transformer. Strict collision-free
success, however, falls from 0.967 at 5 robots to 0.708 at 20, 0.250 at 30,
and 0.000 at 40 for the GNN, with the transformer showing the same shape
(0.983, 1.000, 0.767, 0.242, 0.017). Physical collision events per episode
rise from zero at 5--10 robots to 6.883 for the GNN and 9.025 for the
transformer at 40 robots, and minimum separation collapses from above 0.95~m
to about 0.4~m. Control time grows steadily with population, from 0.890~ms to
2.066~ms for the GNN.

Without the filter the picture is starker: the GNN reaches 0.992--1.000 goal
success at every scale but strict collision-free success of 0.092 at 5 robots
and 0.000 everywhere else, and the transformer never exceeds 0.117. High
arrival rates therefore coexist with near-total failure of the safety
criterion, which is exactly why we report the two separately. We read 10--20
robots as the practical operating range of the current system and treat 30--40
robots as a stress test: adding robots barely degrades goal seeking, but it
overloads the mechanism meant to keep them apart.

\subsection{Seed Stability}
Three independently trained GNN checkpoints give mean success of
$0.517\pm0.388$, $0.767\pm0.161$, and $0.850\pm0.115$ at 5, 10, and 20 robots.
The 5-robot variance is large enough that reporting a single best checkpoint
would be misleading and indicates the present training configuration is not
yet stable at small team sizes. All tables above aggregate over seeds and
checkpoints rather than selecting the best run.

\section{ROS~2 Integration}

A Tkinter two-dimensional interface switches between the ORCA, RVO, A*, MLP,
and GNN controllers and displays robots, goals, communication edges,
trajectories, and live metrics. The ROS~2 adapter targets a Wheeltec model and
was verified across three namespaces on the \texttt{/robot\_i/odom},
\texttt{/robot\_i/scan}, and \texttt{/robot\_i/cmd\_vel} topics, with a marker
node publishing \texttt{/multi\_robot/markers} so RViz2 renders robots, goals,
trails, and graph edges; the launch script is
\texttt{ros2/run\_multi\_robot\_gnn.sh}. This validates the topic contract and
visualization path on a Gazebo model, not physical-robot behavior, and no
real-hardware safety claim is made here.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}

We built and evaluated a dynamic-graph message-passing framework for
multi-robot cooperative navigation, combining five-dimensional
relative-motion edge features, a joint imitation and separation objective, an
external pairwise safety filter, and a verified ROS~2 path. The measurements
support three conclusions. Message passing contributes genuine coordination
ability: the same architecture with edges removed loses roughly 0.30 success
at 10 robots. A denser graph is not automatically better, since the global
graph fails to improve on the local one. Most importantly, filtering and
imitation together deliver high arrival rates at 5--20 robots but cannot hold
the safety criterion at 30--40 robots, where strict collision-free success
collapses to near zero.

Several caveats bound these conclusions. Our RVO-style controller is a
finite-lattice method written for experimental uniformity, not a formally
verified implementation of the RVO reference algorithm, so comparisons
against it should be read accordingly. The main-comparison GNN checkpoint was
trained at 10 robots, making its 5 and 20-robot numbers cross-scale
generalization results rather than matched performance. The imitated expert is
itself rule-based, so the policy inherits its teacher's limitations, and the
Gazebo work demonstrates the interface and visualization chain only rather
than physical-robot behavior. Finally, the present work is imitation-based;
reinforcement fine-tuning with long-horizon progress and hard safety
constraints remains incomplete, and the multi-scale results are the evidence
that it is needed. The clear next steps are therefore safety-constrained
policy training instead of post-hoc projection, a verified RVO baseline,
improved cross-map generalization, and validation on physical robots.
